Chapter 1

What is the Billing System?

It is the software a physical-therapy clinic's billing staff use to turn completed treatments into bills for insurance companies, to chase those bills until they are paid, and to keep track of every step in between.

The problem it solves

When a patient is treated at a clinic, the patient usually does not pay the full price. An pays most of it. But an insurer only pays if the clinic sends a bill that follows strict rules: the right codes, the right identifiers, the right dates, proof that the treatment was approved beforehand, and so on.

If anything is wrong the insurer refuses to pay, often weeks later. Clinics lose real money this way. And because a clinic sends hundreds of bills a month, nobody can check them by hand.

This system sits between the clinic's clinical software and the insurers. It checks every bill before it goes out, sends it, tracks what happens to it, records the money that comes back, and puts everything that needs a human decision into a work list with an owner and a due date.

In one sentence per stage

Goes inFinished treatment notes from the clinic's clinical system, plus the patient's insurance details and the clinic's own setup.
System checksIs anything missing, wrong, or not allowed by this insurer?
Comes outA — the formal bill — sent electronically or printed on paper.
Comes backMoney, a refusal, or silence. Each is handled differently.
ResultPayments recorded, balances updated, unpaid items chased, and figures for management.

Who uses it, and who does not

👤 Uses the system daily

Billing staff. They review charges, fix problems, send claims, post payments, chase insurers and work refusals. In the requirements they are mostly the .

👤 Uses it occasionally

Administrators and owners. They set the clinic up, manage users and permissions, connect clinics to the clinical system, and look at results across clinics.

🏥 Does not use it

Clinicians. Therapists work only in the clinical system. They have no account here at all — but the moment they finish a treatment note, this system starts working. PRD §1.3 · p2

The four outside parties

Four things outside the system shape everything inside it. None of them is a user; all of them send or receive information.

⬛ External

The clinical system where therapists document treatment. It sends finished notes here.

⬛ External

The one principle the requirements put above everything else

Every item that needs a human to do something carries six things: an owner, a status, a priority, a due date, a next action and its history. Nothing should sit in a pile with nobody responsible for it. PRD core principle · p1

The requirements state this as the product's core idea, but the data model in chapter 10 has no fields for owner, priority, due date or next action.

Chapter 3 · the most important diagram

The big picture: one treatment, from note to money

This is the whole system on one page. Follow the thick path down the middle for the normal case; the branches to the sides are what happens when something is wrong, missing or refused. Click any box to read what that word means.

The happy path

A note arrives, nothing is wrong, staff release it, it passes the checks, it goes to the clearinghouse, the payer pays, the money is recorded, the balance is zero. Most visits should do this.

The side paths

Missing data, a provider who is not yet approved by the insurer, no authorisation, an insurer held for manual release, a refusal, or silence. Each side path is a work queue with an owner.

The loops

A held claim returns to the checks by itself once the cause is fixed. A claim in a release bucket moves on only when a person releases it. A refused claim can return as a corrected claim. Nothing is thrown away.

How the roles interact

What a Practice Admin may do, module by module

This is the only role the requirements spell out in detail. "Create / Read / Update / Delete" is the standard way of saying: add new records, look at them, change them, remove them.

Two unresolved points about permissions

Which model applies? Chapter 1 describes permissions set per person, per screen section, in three levels (Edit / View / Hidden), including hiding a single field. Chapter 10 describes permissions attached to a role, per module. The two are different systems of thought.

Which roles exist? Five role names appear across three chapters: Organization Admin, Practice Admin, Domain Admin, System Admin, plus service accounts. Only two are defined.

Chapter 12

Starting from zero

Everything so far described a system that is already busy. A real practice starts with an empty one. This chapter shows what exists on the first day, and why records have to be created in a particular order: each one points at something that must already be there.

What exists on day one

The requirements name exactly one thing that is created in advance: two roles, and . PRD §10.2 · p14

No practice, no insurer, no clinician, no patient, no claim. The requirements do not say whether a list of or diagnosis codes comes pre-loaded, or how the very first user account is created.

What has to exist before what

Read the diagram from the top left. An arrow means “this must exist first”. The top band is set-up work done once, the middle band repeats for each patient, and the bottom bands repeat for every visit. The requirements say what depends on what, but not the order staff should work in, so the numbers are only a sensible order to follow.

Why classes before insurers

Every must belong to exactly one , which supplies its default billing rules. An insurer cannot be added until a class exists. PRD §10.3 · p16

Why the case must be completed

A claim needs a , at least one and a to be addressed to. All three live on the case, so a visit cannot be billed until the case has them. PRD §10.4 · p19

Why a visit needs set-up data

A names where treatment happened and which gave it, and each is one procedure code. Without a location, a provider and a code there is nothing to bill. PRD §10.4 · p19

The system, and notes about it

Inside the prototype, the Billing System screens look and behave like the real product: they never mention the requirements document, assumptions or open questions. What the requirements leave open is attached to the screens as small review notes — a control at the bottom right and numbered markers next to the fields they concern. Each note is one of three kinds: an assumption, a client question, or a short note, with its reference. Hide them and the prototype is the product alone.

Switching environment or resetting asks for confirmation first and starts that environment again from its beginning. Nothing created in one environment appears in the other, and nothing is saved when the page is refreshed.

What the prototype had to assume

To be usable on day one, the Fresh System provides one System Administrator account and loads the standard diagnosis and adjustment-reason code lists, and it opens the current month for each new practice. None of this is stated in the requirements.
